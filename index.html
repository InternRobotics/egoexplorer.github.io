Humanoids are built for human spaces, and almost nothing useful in those spaces happens standing still. A person fetching a reagent walks to the bench, leans in, adjusts their stance so both hands can reach, and keeps the grasp while they turn away. The walking and the manipulation are not two phases. They are one behaviour.

That is the behaviour we want robots to learn from, and it is the behaviour today's portable datasets record least well. Egocentric video scales beautifully and carries enormous visual and semantic breadth, but the body that produced the view is unobserved. Wearable trackers recover the body, but usually stop at the wrist. Glove and contact datasets recover the fingers, or the touch, and usually drop the walking. Each one is a true recording of part of the behaviour, and the part they all drop is the coupling between the parts.

The datasets that do carry body and view together are mostly captured in instrumented space, or carry the body without the hands. We built EgoExplorer to record that coupling: an observatory of human loco-manipulation — 100+ hours and growing, 2,715 recordings — captured with a wearable rig light enough to walk between a chemistry bench, a factory bay and a kitchen. Four streams — what the person saw, how the whole body moved, what every finger did, and where the hands actually pressed — are recorded together, on one clock, in rooms we did not instrument.

    03 / CAPTURE

    Five anchors, two gloves, one camera

    The design target was blunt: everything must mount on the body in a few minutes, survive a day of real work, and leave the wearer able to do their actual job. Anything that needed a tripod, a base station or a clear line of sight was out before we started.

    What remains is deliberately sparse. Five trackers, at the head, the two wrists and the two ankles. Five poses is a fraction of what a motion-capture suit gives you — and that is the trade we took, because five is what a person will actually wear to work.

    THE WHOLE RIG

    Everything rides on the wearer

    Everything mounts on the body. Nothing stands in the room: no tripod, no base station, no line of sight to keep clear.

    HEAD

    Tracker + camera, rigidly coupled

    A GoPro HERO13 with Media Mod is bolted to the head tracker through a printed adapter. The coupling is the point: it turns the camera pose into a measured quantity instead of something to solve for later.

    WRISTS

    Tracker over the glove cuff

    The wrist tracker gives a metric end-effector trajectory; the glove under it gives the fingers. Both glove configurations share one mount layout, so swapping gloves does not invalidate a calibration. The ankles carry the same tracker on a lateral strap.

    The gloves are where the dataset earns its last two streams. MANUS Metagloves give clean finger articulation. Wuji gloves trade a little articulation fidelity for seventeen tactile regions per hand, which is the difference between knowing a hand was near an object and knowing it was pressing on it.

    One more property matters more in the field than any sensor choice. Participant geometry and mount placement are solved once, before any task is recorded, and reused for every recording until the rig comes off. A session costs a donning and a short calibration, not a re-rig.

    Body5 × VIVE Ultimate Tracker — head, wrists, ankles, inside-out, no base stations
    VisionGoPro HERO13 with Media Mod, rigidly coupled to the head tracker
    HandsMANUS Metagloves (articulation) or Wuji gloves (articulation + 17-region tactile)
    MountsCustom 3D-printed, glove dorsum and lateral ankle, strapped
    SetupOne calibration per mounting configuration, reused across every task recording

    A wearing study on the neutral SMPL-X body: the tracker is HTC's own CAD mesh, the camera and Media Mod are illustrative models, and the glove is the Wuji description package. Mount placement is geometric — it shows where each device sits, not a measured calibration.

    04 / RECONSTRUCTION

    From five poses to a whole body

    Sparse sensing moves the cost from hardware to reconstruction, and the bill comes due immediately. A tracker pose is the product of two unknowns — where the device sits on the limb, and how the limb is articulated — and a great many combinations of the two explain the same measurement. Fit them together and a mounting error quietly becomes a pose error. Everything between the five anchors is not observed at all.

    Our pipeline, C³, refuses to solve those unknowns at the same time.

    • Calibrate. A short prescribed sequence, matched by phase against a reference motion library, gives body shape, metric scale and each sensor-to-anchor transform. Solved once per mounting configuration, and fixed before any motion is inferred.
    • Complete. A temporal prior — AvatarPoser extended from three anchors to five and trained on AMASS — turns the calibrated anchors into a first whole-body trajectory, supplying the joints no tracker observes.
    • Constrain. That trajectory is plausible but not yet faithful. Three layers refine it against the measurements, against the body's own surface, and against the floor.

    One real recording — sandwich making, at 30 Hz — replayed inside a stand-in kitchen whose surfaces are placed from the body's own occupancy over the whole clip, so the figure comes right up to them and never through them. Everything else is measured: the five trailing markers are the tracker poses, the body is the solved SMPL-X, and the heat on the hands at the end is the glove's own per-region pressure, region by region. One correction is applied for legibility: the glove fit returns a free rotation at every finger joint, including twist that a finger cannot do, so each joint is projected back onto the motion it has — flexion, and a little abduction at the knuckle.

    Only the body is reconstructed. Finger articulation and contact are measured directly: the glove reports twenty-one hand points and, in the tactile configuration, seventeen regions of pressure per hand, at roughly 120 Hz. The pipeline's job for the hands is alignment, not estimation — the calibrated wrist transform is what carries the glove's own frame onto the solved arm, so the fingers and the pressure land on the same body, on the same clock.

    The third stage is the one that is easy to under-build. Matching a wrist target says nothing about whether the arm passed through the chest on the way, and an ankle above the floor says nothing about the heel. So the surface layer constrains oriented clearance on the participant's own posed mesh, and the ground layer runs over every posed vertex. All three optimise one trajectory through one shared objective, not one after another.

    Against tracker-fitted references this reaches 18.0 mm body-point error, against 64.1 mm for the strongest baseline we evaluated — AvatarPoser retrained on all five anchors. The number we actually watch is the one at the seventeen joints no tracker ever sees: 20.3 mm, barely worse than the average over the whole body. A method that merely snapped to its anchors would show the opposite — tight at the five, loose at the elbows and the knees — so it is that near-flat margin, not the headline figure, that says the articulation itself improved.

    05 / ENVIRONMENTS

    Five rooms, reconstructed

    Because the rig carries no room-side infrastructure, the environment is recovered rather than instrumented. Each capture space is reconstructed once, and every session in it can be replayed inside its own geometry. Each one turns on its own; drag to look around it.

    06 / EMBODIMENT

    Recorded on a person, performed by a humanoid

    A recording earns its keep when a robot can do something with it. Two unedited sessions are mapped here onto a Unitree G1 carrying two five-finger Wuji hands and played back in physics. A whole-body controller keeps the robot on its own feet through the walking, the leaning and the reaching — the balance is the robot's, not a fixed base or a supporting hand.

    1. Retarget. The solved SMPL-X body is mapped onto the G1's 29 joints, root and feet anchored to the floor the person actually walked on.
    2. Balance. A motion-tracking policy trained in MuJoCo physics follows that reference with joint torques, over whole sessions and through pushes.
    3. Hands. The glove's finger articulation drives twenty joints per hand, collision-aware, so opposition and pinches close without passing through.
